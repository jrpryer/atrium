::: page prose | Of Damper Places in General
My first volume treated of the merely damp: the weeping walls of the Ribs, the cistern steps, the north side of every chimney in the Hem. It was well received by both of its readers. I have since been told, kindly, that I spent too long on the chimneys.

This second volume goes lower, and wetter. I mean the places where water does not merely visit but lives: the spray-shadow behind the falls, the gutters of the Atrium, the rims of pools that have not seen full sun since they were dug. Here the mosses grow thick as fleece and green as the inside of a pea pod, and a careless step will cost you a boot.

I have lain on my stomach in every one of these places with a lens and a notebook. I recommend it. The world is very quiet at moss height, and nobody asks you to do anything.

::: page prose | Hush-Moss
Of all the mosses of the lower city, the one I love best is hush-moss, and it is the hardest to love, because you can barely see it.

It grows only in water, and only where the water is cool and shaded: the deep corners of the Atrium pools, under overhangs, where the sun comes late and leaves early. It makes soft cushions just beneath the surface, the colour of wet slate, and it sways when nothing else is moving.

Its peculiar gift is this. It dims the water around it. Not muddies; dims. A pool with a good bed of hush-moss looks a shade darker than its neighbour, as though a small cloud stood over it on a clear day. The Stillkeepers, who let me kneel at their pool edges on condition that I do not hum, say it "draws the light down to sleep". I have found no better explanation in nine years of looking.

Lift it from the water and it goes grey and sulky within the hour. Put it back and it forgives you by morning.
