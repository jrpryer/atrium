::: page prose | Jet
In the Hem we wear jet for grief. You will see it on every second wrist in a mourning house: small beads of black stone, drilled through and strung on black cord, worn until the wearer feels like taking them off, and not a day before.

Good jet is warm to the touch. It is the first thing people notice and the thing they remember afterwards. Glass is cold, and dyed bone is dull, but jet sits warm against the skin, as though somebody had only just been holding it. It takes a high polish, too; an old string, much handled, shines in lamplight like a wet pebble.

It is polite to lend a mourner your own jet if they have none. It is not polite to ask for it back. It will come back on its own when they are ready, usually with a cake.

::: page prose | Other Courtesies
When someone in the Hem dies, the household moves for a week into a mourning house, where the kettle is never allowed to go cold. Neighbours come and go without knocking. Bring something to eat and something to do with your hands: peas to shell, socks to darn. Nobody is expected to talk.

The bereaved do not scrub the red clay off their boots that week. The saying is that the Hem is holding on to them for a little while, and it would be rude to shake it off.

On the last evening the family walks down to the edge of the Atrium and sets a lit candle on a flat stone by the water. Do not blow it out. Do not move it. If it is still burning in the morning, that is thought to be very good luck, and also a waste of a candle, and both may be said aloud.
