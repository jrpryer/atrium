::: page prose | Early Life, Such As It Is
Every biography should begin with a birth. This one cannot. Old Stoic was here before the courtyard had a single flagstone, before the four houses were built, before the first stair was cut into the cliff, and it has kept no records. I tried counting its rings by the method of the Gallery botanists, which involves a borrowed auger and a great deal of apologising, and gave up somewhere past the three hundredth with most of the trunk still to go. My best estimate of its age is: yes.

What I can describe is its character. It is enormous. It leans out over the pools like an old man looking for something he dropped. For as long as anyone has written anything down, it has never once been described as being in a hurry. Storms have come and gone. The falls have thinned. Keepers have come and gone. Old Stoic has carried on, in its own words, saying nothing.

The subject declined to be interviewed for this book.

::: page prose | The Last Waystation
In the old days the Long Stair did not end at the Hem. It ended here. Pilgrims came down from the shrines above the Crown, tier by tier, resting at each waystation tree along the way, and the last of those trees, at the very foot of everything, was Old Stoic. Under its branches they set down their packs, unlaced their boots, and sat a long while in the cool beside the pools, having arrived.

The tree still bears a mark of those days, though not one I can read. All around the trunk, at about the height of a raised hand, the bark is pocked with old square nail-holes. There are dozens of them. They are very old, each one ringed with dark, smooth bark.

I have asked the Keeper, two Stillkeepers, and a pilgrim's great-grandson what they are for. I received a shrug, a long silence, and a recipe for soup.

::: page poetry | To Old Stoic, Who Will Not Read This
You lean as if you meant to cross
the water, and thought better;
you wear the moss, you wear the dusk,
you never write a letter.

The stairs came down, the pilgrims came
and set their burdens by;
they slept beneath your patient arms
and you did not ask why.

The falls grew thin, the lamps grew few,
the Keepers came and went;
you stood, and stood, and stood some more,
and called it time well spent.

So here's your book. It's yours to keep.
I've written all I could.
You'll outlast every word of it,
dear stubborn, silent wood.
