::: page prose | Before
I went down the tiers with a notebook and one question: what did the falls look like, before? Everyone over thirty had an answer, and most of them had two.

"Like a spoon in candlelight," said a laundress in the Ribs. "You'd hang the sheets out and they'd come in with a shine on them. Not much. Enough to feel smug about."

A gardener in the Hem told me the silver settled in her furrows after rain, so that her beans grew up in glitter for a week. "Didn't make them taste any better," she said. "But you looked at them twice."

A glazier remembered lying on the Atrium moss as a boy, watching the water come down "like someone pouring out a whole drawer of forks, slowly, for ever."

Nobody I asked had ever wondered where the shine came from. It belonged to the falls, the way wet belongs to water.

::: page prose | Since the Quiet Water
Then came the Year of Quiet Water, five hundred and forty-one, and the shine went out of it.

Nobody could tell me the day. "It wasn't a day," said a lamp-diver's widow. "It was a summer. You just noticed, one evening, that it had gone turquoise and lazy, like it had sat down."

The falls are still tall. They are still lovely, in a thin and drifting way, the colour of a duck's egg held up to a window. Children born since think that is simply what waterfalls look like, and they are not wrong, exactly.

But the old ones miss it. A cobbler in the Gallery keeps a jar of silver silt he once swept off his own front step. He showed it to me the way you might show someone a letter. "Nineteen years," he said. "You'd think somebody up top would have noticed."

I have written down what they told me. That is all an oral history can do: hold on to the shine until somebody brings it back.
