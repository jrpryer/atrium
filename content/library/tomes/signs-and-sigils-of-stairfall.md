::: page list | The Five Shelving Sigils
Every library in Stairfall marks its shelves
with one of five sigils. Learn them, and you
will never again shelve a book of fish among
the poets.

THE SPRIG .............. Horticulture
  A stem with two leaves. Things that grow.

THE STAIR .............. History
  Three steps going down. Things that happened.

THE RIPPLE ............. Husbandry
  Three rings, one inside another.
  Things we keep alive.

THE OPEN EYE ........... Mysticism
  An eye without a lid. Things not seen at once.

THE PLUMB-LINE ......... Mathematics
  A cord and a weight. Things that can be counted.

Point first. Read the spine second.
Argue with the librarian third, if at all.

::: page prose | The Closed Hand
There is a sixth sign that every librarian must know, and I set it apart from the five on purpose.

It is a closed hand around a flame: the fingers curled, not crushing, the small fire safe inside them. It is the sign of things given to be kept: offerings, tokens, and vows. Where you find it, something has been handed over in trust, to a shrine or a spring or some other keeping, and is meant to stay there until it is called for. Shrines mark such things with the closed hand, and shrines keep them.

Libraries never shelve them. Not on any shelf, under any sigil, however learned the thing looks or however neatly it is labelled. A thing that bears the closed hand is not ours to file. It belongs to whoever gave it, and to whatever it was given to. If one ever turns up among your books, do not shelve it. Set it aside, gently, and ask whose it is.

::: page prose | The Crown's Seal, and Other Civic Signs
The Crown's seal you will have seen on every proclamation, coin-weight and stern letter that ever came down the Stair: the falls, the tree, and four lamps. There is a long argument among the Crown Clerks about what the four lamps stand for. I have sat through it twice and will not repeat it here.

Other civic signs are simpler. The Assayers' Exchange marks its weights with a pair of scales held level, and woe to the merchant whose scales are not. The waterworks of the Gallery Reach mark their gates with three upright bars above a wave. The glassmakers sign their best work with a single teardrop, and their worst with nothing at all, out of modesty.

Point at all of these with confidence. Pointing is half of learning. The other half is asking someone afterwards whether you were right, and minding your manners when they say no.
