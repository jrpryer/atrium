::: page prose | The Tides of the Ribs
The cisterns of the Ribs have tides. Not the sea's tides, which I have read about and privately doubt, but tides of their own: they fill and they empty, slowly, and a person who keeps careful count can tell you what the water will do a week from now.

The arithmetic is simple. What comes in is the water down through the intakes and the rain off the ledges. What goes out is everything the quarter drinks, washes, boils, and spills, and whatever the rock drinks, which is more than you would think. When more comes in than goes out, the water rises. When less, it falls. Everybody knows this. Nobody writes it down. I write it down.

I have read the level of the Great North Cistern off a marked pole every morning for eleven years, in damp socks on every one of those mornings. The lamp-divers tell me the intakes run cleaner when the water is high. My socks have stopped telling me anything.

She would not tell me what her count is worth in hands,
only that water is happiest when nobody argues with it.
