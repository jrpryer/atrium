::: page prose | The Lampstone
Every charter-lamp below the Gallery, every lamp you will ever trim, was first kindled from the same stone. Learn the stone before you learn anything else.

The lampstone is a smooth oval, pale as milk-glass, worn soft at the edges by generations of careful hands. It is cold. Hold it in your bare palm on the hottest afternoon of the year and it stays as cold as a cellar floor. Do not let this alarm you.

Each midsummer the lampstone is carried down from the Crown, tier by tier, to the lamps of the lower city. It drinks the sun at the top of the city and gives it back at the bottom: touch it to a wick and the wick takes light, though the stone itself never warms.

You will know the true stone by its base, where the Crown's charter-seal is cut: the falls, the tree, and four lamps. Anything else offered to you as the lampstone is a pebble with ambitions.

::: page recipe | Gilded Lamp-Oil, for the Lantern
Take good pressed oil, the clearest you can get. Add a measure of patience; there is no shortcut, and those who look for one end up with oil that is merely oil. Add something of true wealth, glittering and rare. Warm it gently, never to the boil, until a faint gold comes into the jar.

Burned in a lamplighter's lantern, gilded oil gives a low, honeyed light, and by that light you may read lamplit ink: the gold-ink that wardens and pilgrims use for their private notes. By ordinary light such writing cannot be seen at all. By gilded lamplight it stands up off the page as plain as a shop sign.

A lamplighter is trusted with this because a lamplighter goes everywhere and reads nothing they ought not. See that you are worthy of the trust. If you must read somebody's private note, at least have the decency to blush.

::: page poetry | The Lamplighter's Song
Down from the Crown on the longest of days,
cold in my pocket though summer's ablaze,
I carry the sun to the tiers that are dark,
a stone in my hand and a wick for a spark.

Gallery, Ribs, and the Hem in its clay,
I give back the light that I carried away:
oil for the lamp and a trim for the wick,
and a word to the moths, who are never too quick.

Last to the Atrium, under the tree,
where the water comes down like a slow cup of tea,
I light the four lamps and I sit for a while,
and the Keeper comes out with a lamp-coloured smile.

Then up all the stairs with my pockets made light,
and the whole of the city lit under the night.
