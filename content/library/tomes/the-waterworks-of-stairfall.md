::: page prose | The Gallery Reach
At the Gallery's edge, where the city's water gathers itself for the long drop, stands the Reach: three great sluices of oak and iron, side by side across the channel, numbered I, II and III from the cliff outward. Every drop that falls to the Atrium passes through them first.

Each sluice is opened or closed with a single great key, long as a forearm, with a bit of solid silver. Silver, because it does not corrode in the spring water as iron would, and a sluice-key must turn as sweetly in its hundredth year as in its first. Each key is held by its own sluice-warden, one warden to one sluice, and by no one else.

When all three sluices stand open together, the full flow comes down from the Hallows springs, and with it a fine silver silt, so that the falls run silver from lip to pool and glitter in any light. With fewer open, the water still falls, but the silt stays behind. The sums are overleaf. They are beautiful.

::: page table | Flows at the Reach
FLOWS AT THE GALLERY REACH
(high summer, in barrels a minute, by my own count)

+--------------+-------+-----------------------------+
| Sluices open | Flow  | The falls                   |
+--------------+-------+-----------------------------+
| None         |     0 | Dry. Unthinkable.           |
| Any one      |   350 | A thread of turquoise.      |
| Any two      |   700 | Tall, turquoise, no shine.  |
| All three    |  1050 | Silver, from lip to pool.   |
+--------------+-------+-----------------------------+

The flow adds up honestly, sluice by sluice. The silver
does not. It comes down only when all three stand open
together, or not at all. An engineer may find this
untidy. A poet would not. I have checked my sums eleven
times, and I remain an engineer.

::: page marginalia faded | In Another Hand
(A blank endpaper at the back of the book. By gilded lamplight, gold writing comes up across it in a small, slanting hand that is not the engineer's.)

Banes forgets to say that the Reach sings in a north wind. It does. Flat.

The table wants a tenth more in spring, for the melt. Take it off again by midsummer.

Along the foot of the page, pressed hard, as if written with cold fingers:

I hold III. When they open the others, I'll open mine, and the falls will run silver, and I will come home. — I.F.
