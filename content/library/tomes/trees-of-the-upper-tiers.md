::: page prose | The Lantern Birch
I begin with the Lantern Birch because it is the reason I bought new boots, and the reason I wore them out.

It is a slender, white-barked tree, and it grows only in the High Hallows, among the shrines and the cold springs above the Crown. It will not root below the Crown. I have carried seedlings down the Long Stair inside my own coat, three springs running, to prove otherwise, and every one had turned brown by the time I reached the Gallery. The tree knows where it lives.

Know it by the leaf. The leaf is pale, the colour of milk held up to a window, and its veins run through it like poured silver, so that a single leaf against the light looks lit from within. Hence the name. Hence also the trouble: the Gallery botanists prize it above all other specimens and press its leaves for their herbaria by the drawerful. I have sold them a few myself. The boots were not cheap.

::: page prose | The Kneeling Juniper
If the Lantern Birch is the poet of the Hallows, the Kneeling Juniper is its doorkeeper. It grows flat to the rock along the windy shoulders of the mountain, never higher than a knee, and it grows sideways out of what I can only call spite. A juniper two hundred years old may be no taller than a footstool and as long as a cart.

The berries are blue-black and bitter. The shrine-keepers dry them for a winter tea which they insist is pleasant. I have drunk it three times and been polite on each occasion.

The juniper's great virtue is that it is always there when you need to sit down. On the steeper stretches of the climb I have rested on it more often than I care to record, and it has never once complained. I recommend it to every out-of-breath botanist as a specimen, a windbreak, and a chair.
